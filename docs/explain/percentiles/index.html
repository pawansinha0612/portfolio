Why averages lie

An average response time of 150 ms can hide thousands of users waiting two seconds. Pick a scenario, or click on the chart to add slow requests, and watch what the average does and what p99 does.

What the numbers mean

  • p50 (median): half of requests were faster than this. It's what a typical request feels like.
  • p95: 1 in 20 requests was slower. A user who loads 20 things on one visit will usually hit at least one of them.
  • p99: 1 in 100 was slower. On a busy shop that's thousands of slow checkouts a day.
  • Average (mean): the total divided by the count. A few very slow requests barely move it, and a bimodal shape (fast cache hits, slow misses) gives an average that no request actually had.

Three rules for reporting latency

  1. Report p95 and p99 next to the median, with the request count. A p99 from 200 requests is two requests' worth of evidence.
  2. Never average percentiles. The average of five servers' p95s is not the p95 of the whole system. Combine the raw data or histograms, then compute.
  3. Set targets on percentiles, not averages: "p95 under 800 ms and errors under 1%" is a test that can actually fail. See percentiles and tail latency in the glossary.

Bonus: when your load tool hides the tail

A test plans one request every 50 ms. Halfway through, the server freezes for 3 seconds. Compare what a closed-loop tool records with what users actually experienced.

Coordinated omission, in one paragraph

A tool that waits for each response before sending the next request slows down exactly when the system does. It "coordinates" with the problem and leaves out the requests that would have been slow. Open-model tests avoid this: k6's constant-arrival-rate and ramping-arrival-rate executors start requests on schedule whatever the server is doing.
