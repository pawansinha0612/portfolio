Error budgets in plain English

      
What the numbers mean

      
What is an error budget?

      
An SLO of 99.9% means one request in a thousand is allowed to fail. That 0.1% is your error budget. Over a 30-day window it's about 43 minutes of full downtime, or the same amount spread across thousands of small failures. The budget turns "is reliability good enough?" into a number product and engineering can both act on: while there's budget left, ship; when it's gone, slow down and fix.

      
How do I pick a target?

      
Start from what users notice, not from what the system does today. Most user-facing APIs land between 99.5% and 99.95%. Every extra nine cuts the budget tenfold: 99.99% gives you about 4 minutes a month, which is less than one bad deploy plus a rollback. Your SLO also can't be higher than the dependencies you call in series.

      
What's a burn rate, and why alert on it?

      
Alerting on "error rate above 0.1%" pages you for blips and misses slow leaks. Burn rate asks how fast the budget is going instead. A burn rate of 14.4 on a 30-day SLO spends 2% of the month's budget in one hour: wake someone up. A burn of 1 sustained for three days spends 10%: raise a ticket. Pairing each long window with a short one (1h with 5m) stops the alert firing long after the problem is fixed.

      
How does this connect to load testing?

      
A load test that passes with a 2% error rate fails a 99.9% SLO twenty times over. Put the SLO in the test as a k6 threshold so the run goes red automatically, then run it in CI before every release. Paste the end-of-test summary into the k6 results explainer to see which requests broke it.

      
Request-based or time-based?

      
This calculator shows both. Time-based ("43 minutes of downtime") is easy to explain; request-based ("10,000 failed requests") is what you actually measure, and it treats a quiet 3am outage and a peak-hour one differently, which matches what users feel.
